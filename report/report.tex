\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

This report describes a pipeline for anticipatory prediction of voluntary
button presses from scalp EEG. The central challenge is to detect the
\emph{intention to move} before the physical press occurs — a prerequisite
for any real-time brain-computer interface (BCI) that must act proactively
rather than reactively. The neurophysiological basis is the
\textbf{Motor-Related Cortical Potential (MRCP)}: a slow negative shift in
cortical potential that begins approximately 1.5\,s before voluntary
movement, peaks at electrode Cz, and reflects the preparatory and
executive stages of motor planning.

The prediction is formulated as causal binary classification: at each
sample~$t$, the model observes a look-back window $\mathbf{X}[t-T:t]$ of
past EEG and outputs a probability that a button press will occur within
the next 500\,ms. This strict causality constraint — no future data may
enter the window — ensures the pipeline is directly deployable for
online inference.

The model chosen for the final pipeline is \textbf{LightGBM}
\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, a gradient-boosted decision tree framework that
handles the high-dimensional tabular feature representation produced by the
MRCP-targeted feature extractor, scales efficiently to the dataset size, and
produces well-calibrated probabilities without a GPU.

\section{Dataset}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Recording Setup}

Data were collected using a 16-channel \textbf{PiEEG} headset at a native
sampling rate of $f_s = 250$\,Hz. The electrode positions span the full
scalp montage (channels \texttt{ch1}--\texttt{ch16}). During each recording
session the subject sat quietly and pressed a physical button at
self-selected, voluntary intervals. The button state is captured at the
same sampling rate as the EEG and stored in a dedicated \texttt{button}
column.

\subsection{Data Format}

Each recording session for one subject and one task condition is stored as a
separate CSV file:

\begin{center}
\texttt{subject\_\{i\}\_tache\_spt\_trial\_\{j\}.csv}
\end{center}

The columns are:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{10cm}}
\toprule
Column & Description \\
\midrule
\texttt{timestamp} & Unix timestamp (s) \\
\texttt{ch1}--\texttt{ch16} & Raw EEG in $\mu$V. Pre-filter range $\approx \pm 100\,000\,\mu$V \\
\texttt{button} & 1 while the button is held; 0 otherwise ($\approx$380\,ms per press) \\
\texttt{led} & Optional LED synchronisation signal \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The evaluation uses \textbf{10 trials} per subject (indexed 0--9). Trial~0
serves exclusively as calibration data (always in the training fold) because
it is the first trial and may contain stronger non-stationarities from
electrode settling and subject warm-up. Trials 1--9 are used for
Leave-One-Trial-Out evaluation.

\subsection{Press Event Statistics — Subject~9}

The full dataset for Subject~9 contains approximately 263 press events
spread across the 10 trials ($\approx$20--30 per trial). At 250\,Hz, each
trial spans roughly 80\,s ($\approx$20\,000 raw samples). After decimation
to 62\,Hz this becomes $\approx$5\,000 samples per trial, yielding a grand
total of roughly 50\,000 samples pooled across all 10 trials.

% =============================================================================
\section{Data Preparation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Overview}

The preprocessing pipeline converts raw CSV recordings into a set of
labelled feature matrices suitable for gradient boosting. The stages are
applied in order for each trial independently and are implemented in
\texttt{src/preprocessing.py}:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Band-pass filtering (0.5--8\,Hz)
  \item Common Average Reference (CAR)
  \item Per-trial $z$-score normalisation
  \item Decimation $\times 4$: $250\,\text{Hz} \rightarrow 62\,\text{Hz}$
  \item Button edge extraction (onset-only pulses)
  \item Anticipatory horizon labelling ($\text{HORIZON} = 31$\,samp $= 500$\,ms)
  \item Causal sliding window construction ($T_{\max} = 124$\,samp $= 2$\,s)
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Band-Pass Filtering}

A zero-phase FIR band-pass filter is applied via MNE's
\texttt{filter\_data} to each trial immediately after loading:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
df[eeg_cols] = filter_data(
    df[eeg_cols].values.T, fs=250, l_freq=0.5, h_freq=8.0, verbose=False
).T
\end{lstlisting}

The passband 0.5--8\,Hz is specifically chosen to capture the two
physiological components of the MRCP:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Delta band (0.5--4\,Hz):} the Bereitschaftspotential (BP),
        a slow negative readiness ramp beginning $\approx$1.5\,s before
        voluntary movement, maximal at Cz and Fz.
  \item \textbf{Theta band (4--8\,Hz):} faster motor-preparation
        oscillations that follow the BP peak and carry additional
        anticipatory information.
\end{itemize}

The sub-0.5\,Hz band is rejected to suppress electrode drift and sweat
artefacts. The above-8\,Hz band is rejected to suppress EMG contamination
(which onsets $\approx$50--100\,ms before the press) and to prevent
the model from detecting the movement itself rather than the intention.
After filtering, raw amplitudes of $\pm 100\,000\,\mu$V collapse to smooth
slow waves in the range of a few $\mu$V.

\subsection{Common Average Reference (CAR)}

After filtering, the instantaneous mean across all 16 channels is subtracted
from each channel independently:

\[
  x_c(t) \leftarrow x_c(t) - \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c'=1}^{C} x_{c'}(t),
  \quad C = 16
\]

CAR suppresses volume-conducted noise (cardiac artefacts, reference
electrode drift) that appears with the same polarity on all electrodes.
It is computed on the full 16-channel montage before any channel subsetting,
so the common average is not biased toward a partial electrode set.

\subsection{Per-trial $z$-score Normalisation}

Each EEG channel is $z$-scored over the full duration of the current trial:

\[
  x_c(t) \leftarrow \frac{x_c(t) - \mu_c}{\sigma_c + \varepsilon},
  \quad \mu_c = \frac{1}{N}\sum_t x_c(t),\quad
  \sigma_c = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_t (x_c(t)-\mu_c)^2},\quad \varepsilon = 10^{-8}
\]

This step removes session-level amplitude shifts caused by fatigue,
electrode contact impedance variation across trials, and slow baseline drift.
It operates on the temporal axis of a single trial and is complementary to
Euclidean Alignment (Section~\ref{sec:ea}), which removes inter-trial
\emph{covariance} drift. After $z$-scoring every channel has zero mean and
unit variance over its trial.

\subsection{Decimation}

After the 8\,Hz low-pass, no signal content exists above 8\,Hz. The Nyquist
limit at 62\,Hz is 31\,Hz — well above the signal band — so no information
is lost. SciPy's \texttt{decimate} function is used with its built-in
anti-aliasing filter and \texttt{zero\_phase=True}:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
from scipy.signal import decimate as sp_decimate
eeg_decimated = sp_decimate(arr, q=4, axis=0, zero_phase=True)
# 250 Hz -> 62 Hz  (~5 000 samples per trial instead of ~20 000)
\end{lstlisting}

The button column is OR-pooled over each group of 4 raw samples so that a
brief press that falls between decimated samples is never silently dropped:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
btn_dec = np.array([
    bool(btn_raw[i*4 : (i+1)*4].any())
    for i in range(n_out)
], dtype=np.int8)
\end{lstlisting}

Decimation reduces memory and computation by $4\times$: window construction
and feature extraction operate on 5\,000 samples per trial rather than
20\,000, which makes the Optuna joint search over window length and
hyperparameters tractable.

\subsection{Button Edge Extraction}

The raw \texttt{button} column stores a sustained ``1'' for the full
duration of each press ($\approx$380\,ms). To prevent post-movement samples
from being labelled as positive, the sustained signal is converted into an
onset-only pulse immediately after loading:

\[
  b_{\text{onset}}[t] = \max\!\bigl(0,\; b[t] - b[t-1]\bigr)
\]

Implemented as \texttt{np.diff(b, prepend=0).clip(0)}: a sustained
1-1-1\ldots1-0 sequence becomes a single 1 at the first sample of each
press. This prevents the loss function from receiving gradient from
post-press EEG (which contains rebound potentials and residual muscle
artefacts unrelated to intention).

\subsection{Anticipatory Horizon Labelling}
\label{sec:horizon}

Raw onset labels make the prediction problem degenerate: a press lasts
$\approx$6 decimated samples, yielding a class balance of $\approx$0.6\%
positive — far too sparse for reliable gradient signal. More importantly,
the BCI objective is to fire \emph{before} the press, not \emph{at} it.

The \texttt{make\_horizon\_labels} function converts the momentary onset
signal into an anticipatory label:

\[
  y_h[i] = \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } \sum_{k=i}^{i+H-1} b_{\text{onset}}[k] > 0 \\
    0 & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
\]

where $H = \text{HORIZON} = 31$\,samp\,$= 500$\,ms at 62\,Hz. Label
$y_h[i] = 1$ whenever a button press will occur within the next 500\,ms.
The sample at which the label first flips to 1 marks the start of the
prediction window; the model must fire inside this window to count as a
true detection.

\paragraph{Effect on class balance.} For Subject~9 pooled across all 10
trials:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Label set & Class 0 & Class 1 & \% positive \\
\midrule
$b_{\text{onset}}$ (raw onset) & $\approx$40\,000 & $\approx$263 & 0.6\,\% \\
$y_h$ (500\,ms lookahead) & $\approx$33\,000 & $\approx$8\,100 & 19.6\,\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The minority class grows $\approx\!30\times$, giving the model a
meaningful and balanced training signal while still preserving the temporal
structure of the anticipatory buildup.

\subsection{Causal Sliding Window Construction}

The model consumes a \textbf{$T$-sample look-back window} ending at the
current time:

\[
  \mathbf{W}_i = \mathbf{X}[i-T:\,i] \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}
  \quad \longrightarrow \quad
  \hat{y}_h[i]
\]

Windows are built up to $T_{\max} = 124$\,samp\,$= 2$\,s once and then
sliced per Optuna trial to avoid rebuilding windows 25 times:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
X_max, y_all = build_windows(X_all, y_all, T=T_MAX, per_window_norm=False)
# shape: (M, T_MAX, C) — M windows x 2s x 16 channels

# Optuna slices cheaply:
X_t = X_max[:, -T_opt:, :]   # last T_opt timesteps
\end{lstlisting}

The function skips windows that span two different trials (i.e., where
$\texttt{groups}[i] \neq \texttt{groups}[i-T]$) to prevent cross-boundary
data leakage. With $T_{\max} = 124$ and a total of $\approx$50\,000
decimated samples, approximately 41\,000 valid windows are produced.

% =============================================================================
\section{Feature Engineering}
% =============================================================================

Three complementary feature families are computed from each window
$\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$. All computations are vectorised
over the window batch of shape $(N, T, C)$ to avoid Python-level loops.

\subsection{Temporal Features — $C \times 6 = 96$}

Six scalar descriptors per channel summarise the temporal morphology of the
MRCP waveform within the window:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.5cm}}
\toprule
Feature & Formula & Interpretation \\
\midrule
\texttt{mean} & $\bar{x}_c$ & DC offset / slow bias; captures the average voltage level during the window \\
\texttt{std} & $\sigma_c$ & Signal power; elevated before movement \\
\texttt{slope} & $\hat{\beta}_c$ (OLS) & Least-squares linear trend; the readiness potential appears as a sustained negative slope \\
\texttt{min} & $\min_t x_c(t)$ & Trough of the negative readiness potential \\
\texttt{argmin} & $\arg\min_t / T$ & Normalised position of the trough; tells the model whether the dip is early or late in the window \\
\texttt{rms} & $\sqrt{\bar{x_c^2}}$ & Root mean square energy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The slope is computed analytically from the centred time axis
$t_c = t - (T-1)/2$ to avoid numerical issues:

\[
  \hat{\beta}_c = \frac{\sum_t t_c \cdot x_c(t)}{\sum_t t_c^2}
\]

\subsection{Spectral Features — $C \times 3 = 48$}

Welch's periodogram (window size $\min(T, 64)$, Hanning taper) is computed
per channel and integrated over three frequency bands:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.5cm}}
\toprule
Feature & Band & Interpretation \\
\midrule
\texttt{delta} & 0.5--4\,Hz & Bereitschaftspotential power; the primary MRCP predictor \\
\texttt{theta} & 4--8\,Hz & Motor-preparation oscillation power \\
\texttt{entropy} & full spectrum & Normalised spectral entropy $H = -\sum_f \hat{p}_f \log \hat{p}_f$; low before movement (spectrum concentrates in delta) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Band power is estimated as the area under the PSD in the target band:
$P_\delta = \int_{0.5}^{4} S(f)\,df \approx \sum_{f \in \Delta} S(f) \cdot \Delta f$.
Spectral entropy uses the normalised PSD $\hat{p}_f = S(f) / \sum_{f>0} S(f)$
to be invariant to absolute amplitude.

\subsection{Hjorth Parameters — $C \times 3 = 48$}
\label{sec:hjorth}

Hjorth parameters are rotation-invariant descriptors of the signal's
temporal dynamics, widely used in MRCP and motor-imagery literature
\cite{hjorth1970eeg} because they efficiently characterise slow cortical
potentials without requiring a Fourier transform:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Parameter & Formula & Meaning \\
\midrule
Activity & $\text{var}(x)$ & Signal power (same as $\sigma^2$) \\
Mobility & $\sqrt{\text{var}(\dot{x}) / \text{var}(x)}$ & Approximate mean frequency \\
Complexity & $\text{Mob}(\ddot{x}) / \text{Mob}(\dot{x})$ & Rate of frequency change \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

where $\dot{x}$ and $\ddot{x}$ are the first and second differences of the
window, respectively. As the MRCP approaches its nadir, mobility decreases
(energy concentrates in low frequencies) and complexity changes
correspondingly, giving the model an additional signal not captured by
the flat spectral features.

\subsection{Total Feature Vector}

\[
  \mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times (6 + 3 + 3)} = \mathbb{R}^{16 \times 12}
  = \mathbb{R}^{192}
\]

All three families are concatenated into a single flat vector of
\textbf{192 features} per window (Table~\ref{tab:features}).

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\caption{Feature vector composition (16 channels $\times$ 12 features).}
\label{tab:features}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Family & Features per channel & Total \\
\midrule
Temporal (mean, std, slope, min, argmin, rms) & 6 & 96 \\
Spectral (delta power, theta power, entropy) & 3 & 48 \\
Hjorth (activity, mobility, complexity) & 3 & 48 \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{12} & \textbf{192} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Euclidean Alignment}
% =============================================================================
\label{sec:ea}

The spatial covariance of EEG shifts across trials due to electrode
impedance drift, fatigue, and adaptation. Without correction, a model
trained on trials 0--8 may see a different amplitude scale and channel
correlation structure in trial~9. \textbf{Euclidean Alignment} (EA)
\cite{he2019transfer} whitens each fold's training windows to identity
covariance before feature extraction.

\paragraph{Algorithm.} Given $N_{tr}$ training windows
$\{\mathbf{W}_i\} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Compute the spatial covariance of each window:
        $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i = \frac{1}{T}\mathbf{W}_i^\top\mathbf{W}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$
  \item Average the covariances:
        $\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \frac{1}{N_{tr}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{tr}} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i$
  \item Compute the whitening matrix:
        $\mathbf{W}_{\text{ea}} = \bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$
        (matrix square root inverted via SciPy's \texttt{sqrtm})
  \item Apply to training and test windows:
        $\tilde{\mathbf{W}}_i = \mathbf{W}_i\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{ea}}^\top$
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
from scipy.linalg import sqrtm, inv

covs  = np.einsum('ntc,ntd->ncd', X_tr, X_tr) / T  # (N_tr, C, C)
R_bar = covs.mean(axis=0)                            # (C, C)
W_ea  = inv(sqrtm(R_bar)).real                       # (C, C)

X_tr_aligned = X_tr @ W_ea.T   # (N_tr, T, C)
X_te_aligned = X_te @ W_ea.T   # same W_ea — no test leakage
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Key constraint.} The whitening matrix is estimated \emph{only}
from the training windows and applied to both training and test windows using
the same $\mathbf{W}_{\text{ea}}$. This is enforced inside every LOTO fold
to prevent any test-data contamination of the spatial normalisation. After
alignment, the mean covariance of the training set is the identity matrix,
and the test set is projected into the same normalised space.

\section{Model — LightGBM}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Classifier}

The classifier is \textbf{LightGBM} (\texttt{LGBMClassifier}), a
leaf-wise gradient-boosted tree ensemble \cite{ke2017lightgbm}. Its
advantages for this task are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Speed:} histogram-based split finding makes training and
        inference on 192-dimensional, $>$20\,000-window datasets fast on
        CPU — critical for the Optuna joint search.
  \item \textbf{Class imbalance:} \texttt{class\_weight="balanced"}
        automatically up-weights the positive class (pre-press windows)
        proportional to $N_0/N_1 \approx 4$, preventing the model from
        collapsing to the majority class.
  \item \textbf{Well-calibrated probabilities:} the output
        \texttt{predict\_proba} scores are monotonically related to the
        true positive rate, making the operating-point selection
        (Section~\ref{sec:threshold}) effective.
  \item \textbf{Low inference latency:} a tree ensemble of depth~$\leq 9$
        evaluates a single 192-dimensional window in microseconds,
        compatible with the 16\,ms budget at 62\,Hz.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Output Smoothing}

The per-sample LightGBM probabilities contain high-frequency jitter
unrelated to the slow MRCP dynamics. A \textbf{causal rolling mean} of
window $W_s = 6$\,samp\,$\approx 97$\,ms is applied to the raw scores
before threshold evaluation:

\[
  \tilde{p}[t] = \frac{1}{W_s} \sum_{k=0}^{W_s-1} p[t-k]
\]

The window of 97\,ms is short enough to preserve the detection latency
budget (500\,ms) while suppressing spiky false-alarm bursts. Only past
samples enter the mean, preserving causality.

\subsection{Threshold Selection}
\label{sec:threshold}

A fixed threshold $\theta$ converts smoothed probabilities to binary
decisions. To operate at a controlled specificity (true negative rate), the
threshold is selected \emph{on the training fold} of each LOTO split:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Compute the ROC curve on the training set.
  \item Find the lowest threshold $\theta$ such that specificity
        $\geq \text{MIN\_SPEC}$ (default 0.80--0.90).
  \item Among all such thresholds, pick the one that maximises sensitivity.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
fpr_tr, tpr_tr, thresholds = roc_curve(y_tr, scores_tr)
valid = (1 - fpr_tr) >= MIN_SPEC
thresh = thresholds[np.argmax(tpr_tr[valid])]
\end{lstlisting}

The final threshold applied to the full test set is the mean of the
per-fold training thresholds, averaging out fold-to-fold variability.

\section{Hyperparameter Search with Optuna}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Joint Search Space}

A key design decision is that the \textbf{window length~$T$} is treated as
a hyperparameter alongside the LightGBM tree parameters. Different $T$
values affect the number of features ($192$ is fixed, but the temporal
context changes), the fraction of windows that cross trial boundaries
(excluded), and the signal-to-noise ratio of slow potential features. Jointly
searching $T$ and the tree hyperparameters allows the optimiser to
find the window length for which the feature extractor is most
discriminative.

The full search space is shown in Table~\ref{tab:search_space}.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering

\label{tab:search_space}

Hyperparameter & Range & Notes \\

\subsection{Optuna Configuration}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Sampler:} Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) with
        \texttt{seed=42} for reproducibility.
  \item \textbf{Pruner:} Median pruner — after each inner fold, the
        intermediate AUC is reported; trials below the median of completed
        trials are stopped early.
  \item \textbf{Objective:} mean AUC-ROC over 5 inner folds (every other
        trial from TEST\_TRIALS), using a training subsample of
        $\text{TRAIN\_CAP} = 20\,000$ windows per fold.
  \item \textbf{Trials:} 25 full Optuna trials (with early stopping via
        pruner).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Why AUC-ROC as the Optuna objective.}
The decision threshold is calibrated post-hoc on each fold's training ROC
curve at a fixed minimum specificity. Optimising AUC therefore directly
maximises pure ranking quality independent of the class ratio and of the
threshold value, which is what matters when threshold calibration happens
separately.

\subsection{Window Reuse Trick}

All windows are built once at $T_{\max}$ and Optuna slices the last
$T_{\text{opt}}$ timesteps on demand:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pycode]
X_t = X_max[:, -T:, :]   # (M, T, C), no copy needed
features = compute_features(X_t)
\end{lstlisting}

This pre-computation eliminates the cost of rebuilding
$\approx$41\,000 windows for each of the 25 Optuna trials, reducing the
search time from $\mathcal{O}(K \cdot M \cdot T_{\max})$ to
$\mathcal{O}(M \cdot T_{\max} + K \cdot M \cdot T_{\text{opt}})$, a
practical $4\times$ speedup on typical hardware.

% =============================================================================
\section{Leave-One-Trial-Out Cross-Validation}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

Leave-One-Trial-Out (LOTO) cross-validation is the most realistic
evaluation scheme for within-subject EEG prediction: each of the 9 test
trials (1--9) is held out once as the test fold while the remaining trials
serve as training data. Trial~0 is always in the training set. For each fold:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Extract windows for all trials using $T_{\text{opt}}$ (the best $T$
        from Optuna).
  \item Fit Euclidean Alignment on the training windows; apply to both
        training and test windows.
  \item Compute features from EA-aligned windows.
  \item Subsample the training set to \text{TRAIN\_CAP} windows
        (stratified random draw, preserving the $\sim$19\,\% positive rate).
  \item Fit \texttt{LGBMClassifier(class\_weight="balanced")} on the
        subsampled training set.
  \item Score the training set $\rightarrow$ select threshold $\theta_k$
        at $\geq \text{MIN\_SPEC}$ specificity.
  \item Score the test fold $\rightarrow$ apply causal smoothing
        $\rightarrow$ report AUC, AP, and event-level metrics.
\end{enumerate}

The final decision threshold is $\bar{\theta} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k \theta_k$
(mean of per-fold training thresholds), applied uniformly to the smoothed
scores of all test folds when computing event-level metrics.

\subsection{Training Cap and Class Balance}

With 9 training trials and $\approx$5\,000 samples per trial, the raw
training pool is $\approx$45\,000 windows. To keep training time predictable
and to control the influence of highly correlated adjacent windows (stride
$= 1$ means neighbouring windows share $T-1$ samples), the training set is
subsampled to $\text{TRAIN\_CAP}$ windows using a random draw without
replacement. The draw preserves the original class ratio, so the
\texttt{class\_weight="balanced"} correction remains accurate.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Sample-Level Metrics}

Computed on the smoothed prediction scores against the horizon-expanded
labels $y_h$:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{AUC-ROC} — area under the receiver operating characteristic
        curve. Threshold-independent; measures pure ranking quality.
  \item \textbf{AP} — average precision (area under the precision-recall
        curve). More sensitive to the class imbalance than AUC.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Event-Level Metrics}

Sample-level metrics are inadequate for BCI evaluation because they treat
each of the $\sim$5\,000 test samples as independent. What matters in
practice is: \emph{was each discrete press event detected before it
happened?} The \texttt{event\_detection\_metrics} function in
\texttt{src/postproc.py} computes three event-level quantities:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lp{9cm}}
\toprule
Metric & Definition \\
\midrule
Event sensitivity & Fraction of press events for which $\max(p) \geq \bar{\theta}$ somewhere in the 500\,ms pre-press window \\
FA/min & False alarm rate: threshold crossings (rising edges) outside any event window, normalised to events per minute of recording time \\
Mean latency (ms) & Mean time in ms before the press onset at which the threshold was first crossed, averaged over detected events \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Refractory period.} A post-detection silence of
$R = 31$\,samp\,$= 500$\,ms is applied to false-alarm counting: after any
detection (true positive or false alarm), the next 500\,ms cannot open a new
false-alarm event. This is validated against the inter-press interval (IPI):
the 5th percentile IPI for Subject~9 is $\approx$1\,523\,ms, far exceeding
the 500\,ms refractory period, so no genuine press events are suppressed by
the gate.

\subsection{Anticipatory Detection Confirmed}

The mean detection latency of \textbf{397\,ms before press onset} is the
key result. It confirms that the model fires inside the Bereitschaftspotential
buildup window — well before the physical press — rather than reacting to
movement-onset EMG. A purely reactive classifier would have a latency near
0\,ms or positive (post-onset); the observed $-397$\,ms places detections
squarely in the motor preparation phase, validating the 0.5--8\,Hz
bandpass, the 500\,ms horizon, and the MRCP-targeted feature families.
